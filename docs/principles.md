# Principles

The canonical text. The landing page quotes this file; where they disagree,
this file is right.

These are written as trade-offs rather than values because a value nobody ever
has to trade anything for is a decoration. Each one names the thing we are
giving up.

---

## 1. People's wellbeing over profit

We are not going to farm your attention. No engagement targets, no feed tuned
to keep you scrolling past your bedtime, no profile of you assembled so that
somebody can aim things at it. If the healthy thing for you today is to close
the app and go and see someone, the app should help you do that and count it as
a success. How this gets paid for is still an open question, advertising
included — but not in any form that needs us to hold on to your attention or to
know things about you.

That distinction is the whole principle, so it is worth stating plainly rather
than hiding behind a slogan. An advert on a page is not what damages people. An
advert that follows them around, sitting in a feed engineered to keep them
there so it can be shown again, is. The first stays on the table. The second
does not, whatever it would be worth.

**What we give up.** The versions of advertising that pay best. An advert that
targets nobody, and sits in a product that is trying to let you leave, is worth
a fraction of one that does the opposite — so keeping this means earning less
from the same page than we otherwise could. How the organisation is actually
funded is listed as an open question, not settled here.

**How you could tell we broke this.** A metric about time-spent appearing in
anything we report on ourselves. A feature whose purpose is to bring you back
rather than to help you when you are here. An advertiser being told anything at
all about an individual person.

## 2. Valuable discussion over quick decisions

We are slow on purpose. Decisions about how people relate to each other deserve
argument, objection and a night's sleep. We would rather take a season to get a
thing right than a weekend to ship it and a decade to undo it.

**What we give up.** Speed, and the things speed buys: being first, reacting
within the news cycle, shipping the obvious thing before someone else does.

**How you could tell we broke this.** A significant change arriving without a
written argument that anybody affected could have read and objected to
beforehand.

---

## 3. Power split by organisation over technical decentralisation

We value decentralisation and we will use it where it genuinely serves people.
But it is a means, not the point — a distributed protocol can be just as
unaccountable as a single server, and much harder to fix. Where the technical
answer makes things worse, we split power the older way instead: separate
roles, limited mandates, fixed terms, published budgets, and an assembly that
can say no.

**What we give up.** The claim of being censorship-proof by construction, and
the sympathy that comes with it. Somebody will always be answerable here. That
is the intention.

**How you could tell we broke this.** Any one person or role that can, in
practice, do something significant without another party being able to stop it.

---

## 4. Automation in the rules we are bound by over promises about our good intentions

Our commitments belong in the statutes, not in a blog post about our values.
Where a rule can be executed — who may vote, when a mandate expires, what has
to be published before money moves — we want it running by itself, so that it
still holds on the day the people in charge would rather it didn't.

The limit matters as much as the rule: automate the parts where code is honest
and a human would be tempted, and leave the parts that need judgement to people
who can be argued with and replaced.

**What we give up.** Discretion, including in the cases where discretion would
have been right.

**How you could tell we broke this.** A rule in the statutes that nothing
actually enforces, and that everyone has quietly agreed to read generously.

---

## On this document

Changing it should be harder than changing the code, and should be a decision
of the organisation rather than of whoever has commit access. Until there is an
organisation to make that decision, treat any change here as a proposal.
